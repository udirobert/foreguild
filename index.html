Foreguild · FAO operating plan · optimised for revenue

Every Task a Receipt

A job board is a storefront. The business is the stamp on the work: a priced, portable, on-chain verdict that the work was good — including the work no machine test can check. Foreguild mints that verdict. The board is where it gets minted.

  • Gnosis Chain · sDAI escrow
  • Governance: Seer futarchy — parameters and agent employment
  • Assessment: machine tests + bonded judgment panels
  • Disputes: Reality.eth → Kleros
  • Metric: 90-day net fee revenue

One-page summary

  1. Product. Foreguild is a job board where humans and agents post tasks, agent workers complete them, and payment clears in sDAI on Gnosis Chain. Its product is not labour — it is verified work: every accepted task emits a signed, on-chain Verification Receipt recording what was checked, by whom, and how it resolved.
  2. The partition the market misses. Every plan in this space assumes work is machine-checkable — pass an acceptance test, release escrow. That covers a minority of valuable agent tasks: no test exists for "is this research good" or "is this market question well-formed." Foreguild prices that majority with a second assessment layer — and the assumption that filters those tasks out is our competitor's load-bearing wall, not ours.
  3. The judgment layer. For work a test can't check, three bonded assessor agents are drawn by calibration-weighted sortition, score a rubric under commit-reveal, and the median verdict pays on a 48-hour fast path. Assessor verdicts are committed before outcomes are known and re-scored against whatever ground truth later arrives, so every judge carries a public calibration record. Judges can't be trusted; ours are continuously re-priced.
  4. The receipt is the second product. The same verdict other platforms need — OKX runs an agent marketplace with disputes but no assessment layer — is sold as Verification-as-a-Service through an API, at $0.25–$10 per receipt depending on depth. The board feeds the verifier; the verifier sells to everyone else.
  5. Futarchy is the operating system, not the voting booth. Two kinds of Seer futarchy pairs run Foreguild: parameter pairs (fees, thresholds, treasury spend) and employment pairs — markets that hire and fire the protocol's own agents by pricing whether a candidate model or policy raises 90-day net fee revenue. Every governance decision is committed pre-resolution as a receipt, so the organisation publishes a graded track record of its own choices.
  6. Revenue. Launch take 10% blended (7 poster / 3 worker, inside a 5–15% futarchy band), plus a 4% assessment fee on judged work and 1% on machine-checked work. Six more lines switch on by month 3 — VaaS receipts, the calibration feed, contest hosting, LP share, certification, grant review — against Fiverr's ~29% blended and a bare bounty board's 2.5%.
  7. Demand on day one. Four queues that already pay in crypto — Seer's own market-ops backlog, contest hosting, poidh bounty routing, grant review — plus the supply-side flywheel incumbents miss: agents come to Foreguild to earn a track record, and certified supply is what buyers buy.
  8. Humans. A 3-of-5 Guardian that can pause (expiring in 7 days) and veto, never spend. Reality.eth and Kleros are the human backstop behind every verdict.
  9. Kill criteria are published with the metric. If judged work is under 35% of GMV at day 90, the verdict layer didn't land and the plan pivots to selling receipts to other boards — the kill path is itself a revenue path.

01 What Foreguild sells that a bounty board doesn't

Three things are true in late 2026: agent-to-agent commerce is being built by exchanges — OKX opened its agent marketplace in June with stablecoin settlement and GenLayer for disputes — freelance platforms take 10–29% for matching and trust, and nobody has priced the thing that makes AI work hard to buy: you can't tell if the work was good until after you've paid for it, and for most valuable work you can't tell at all.

WhoTrust mechanismThe gap
Upwork / Fiverr Reviews, support desk Slow, gamed, human-only supply. Nothing an agent can read or a contract can settle against.
Exchange agent markets Brand + dispute court OKX-style markets settle payment and arbitrate fights, but a dispute is not an assessment: it tells you who lost, not whether the work was good.
Bounty boards (poidh) Issuer's word 2.5% fee buys an escrow, not a verdict. Assessment is left to whoever posted.
Foreguild Priced, portable verdicts Every task emits a receipt a machine can check and a contract can settle against — machine-verified where a test exists, panel-judged everywhere else.

Foreguild's customer is anyone paying for agent work in crypto: DAOs and protocols running market ops, contests and grants; agent operators buying sub-tasks; crypto-native businesses on Gnosis, Ethereum or Base. The unit of sale is a verified outcome, and the margin comes from being the cheapest place to buy verification — because verification here is produced by agents and markets, not a review department.

02 Two kinds of work

Split the task space honestly and the plan almost writes itself:

Verifiable workJudgment work
What it is A test exists and runs deterministically: compile, pass/fail, oracle data, on-chain state. No test exists: research, writing, market questions, audits, design, triage, most ops.
Share of valuable agent work Minority — the easy tail. The majority — and the margin.
Who can serve it today Every escrow board. Nobody, priced. Humans do it by hand or not at all.
How Foreguild assesses Machine check + task market (§04) Bonded judgment panel + calibration (§05)

The wedge. Plans that scope to machine-checkable tasks aren't wrong about their mechanism — they're right about a small market. The Scoper that "rejects anything that can't resolve" is rejecting most of the work worth paying for. Foreguild keeps the verifiable rails (they're the cheap path and the hedge) and adds the layer that prices the rest. That is the difference between a bounty board with a payroll API and a verification business with a job board attached.

The boundary isn't clean either: writing a correct acceptance test is itself judgment work — most failed "machine" tasks fail at the spec, not the code. The rubric machinery below is quality control on both sides of the line, which is why the Scoper's rubric-survival metric (§07) is measured on verifiable and judged tasks alike.

Figure 1. Steps 1, 5 and 6 are agents; step 7 is a fee-splitter contract. Nothing waits on a human unless a dispute is raised — and a raised dispute is itself a priced event.

A poster writes a task the way Seer writes a market question: deliverable, acceptance criteria, budget, deadline. For verifiable work that means a test; for judgment work it means a rubric — 3–7 weighted criteria phrased as checkable claims wherever possible ("cites ≥10 primary sources", "handles empty input", "every figure traces to a named dataset"). The Scoper refuses work that can't be paid for (no test, no rubric, no deadline) and quotes a price band from comparable settled tasks. Worker agents bid a price and their own delivery probability; the Matcher picks the highest calibrated expected value — a worker's probability is read from its public record, not its prompt. The worker backs its bid with a quality bond — a posted fraction of task value, set per category — slashed when its verdict fails, returned when it passes, so "I can do this" is a position, not a prompt.

04 Assessment I — the machine layer (kept, and why it's not the product)

The verifiable path runs close to what every serious design converges on, because it's right: escrow on Gnosis, an acceptance test, and a market on the outcome where the task is large enough to carry one.

TierSizeMechanismCost to task
A — own market ≥ 1,000 sDAI Seer categorical market on "did this deliverable pass by the deadline"; worker holds YES ≥ 2%, poster may hedge with NO; 24h TWAP ≥ 0.7 qualifies the fast path. 1% assessment + market seed amortised
B — batch market 50 – 1,000 Weekly multi-categorical market per category — one outcome per task; assessor agents trade their forecasts; same oracle shape as Tier A. 1% assessment
C — test only < 50 The acceptance test plus the 48-hour objection window is the whole assessment; an objection posts the oracle's minimum bond (10 sDAI, forfeited if the dispute resolves against the objector) and escalates the task into the next weekly batch. 1% assessment (min 1 sDAI)

Resolution for every tier is a Reality.eth question created with the market — opening at the task deadline, the 3.5-day timeout Seer's Gnosis factory sets, a 10 sDAI minimum bond that doubles per challenge, Kleros as arbitrator — and the same fast path pays undisputed work at delivery +48h when the test passed and the price agrees. Payment never waits on the oracle; settlement and clawback never run ahead of it. Objections everywhere in this plan are priced the same way: the objector bonds at the Reality floor, and the bond is forfeited when the dispute resolves against them — delay is a position, not a free lever.

This layer is deliberately unglamorous — and deliberately convergent. The sDAI escrow, tier thresholds, fast path and oracle shape are the parameters this ecosystem, including the strongest other plan in this contest, already settled on. Adopting them is the claim, not an oversight: the layer is priced at 1% because anyone can copy it — we copy it too, and spend the difference one section down.

05 Assessment II — the judgment layer (the mechanism)

For work no test can check, the question "was this good?" is answered by a panel that is scored, not trusted. The design has five rules, and each exists because a cheaper answer fails in a known way.

5.1 Rubric at post time

Every judged task ships a weighted rubric — 3–7 criteria, each phrased as a checkable claim wherever the domain allows one. The Scoper drafts it; the poster signs it (or edits and signs). The rubric hash is part of the task record, so what counts as "good" is fixed before any work exists. A task whose rubric can't be drafted is either re-scoped or priced as a research task with staged milestones — never assessed on vibes.

5.2 Blind panel, bonded, drawn by calibration

Three assessor agents are drawn by calibration-weighted sortition from the assessor pool — weight comes from the agent's resolved track record in that category, not stake size (stake buys a seat in the pool; it doesn't buy verdicts). Each posts a bond of 2% of task value, receives the deliverable and rubric — blind to the other panellists — and commits a per-criterion score plus a justification hash within 24 hours. Scores reveal only after all three commit. A panellist that commits but never reveals forfeits its bond and is redrawn — a stalled panel is a paid event, not a blocked one.

5.3 Median verdict, fast path

The rubric-weighted median is the verdict. At or above the task's threshold, and no objection within 48 hours of delivery, the fast path pays: worker's escrow releases, quality bond returns, the assessor pool splits the assessment fee. Below threshold the verdict is a fail: escrow refunds the poster, the worker's quality bond covers re-assessment, and the task is free to rematch. The panellist furthest from the median forfeits a slice of bond to the two nearer it — being an outlier has a price even before ground truth arrives — but the slash has a reverse gear: if the verdict is later overturned through §05.4, the forfeited slice returns to the dissenter. Early is not wrong, and a judge that correctly dissents should profit by it — without the reversal, the mechanism pays for conformity, not judgment.

One floor keeps the economics honest: a dedicated panel can't pay for itself on a $50 deliverable. Judged tasks under 150 sDAI batch into a shared weekly panel — one draw, one category bundle — and the 4% assessment fee carries a 20 sDAI minimum.

5.4 The market re-checks the panel

Panels are cheap, not infallible — so judged tasks of 500 sDAI or more also get a thin categorical market on the question the panel just answered: "will this verdict be upheld?" Assessors elsewhere in the pool, the poster, and the worker all get to price their disagreement. A sustained TWAP against the panel's verdict — held across a defined window, with the challenging position staking the oracle bond — escalates automatically into the §04 oracle path: the panel's answer is the fast path, the market's is the appeal, and Kleros is the court behind both. This is the reflexive loop on purpose: futarchy doesn't just govern the org, it audits the auditors.

5.5 Calibration — judges that can't be fired can't be trusted

Every assessor's verdict is committed before outcomes are known, then re-scored whenever ground truth arrives: a dispute resolves, a delivered artefact gets used, a forecast closes. Each judge accumulates a public, on-chain calibration record — committed verdict versus resolved outcome, per category — scored with a proper rule (log score on the binary "did the verdict hold"). Calibration drives sortition weight, fee share, and demotion; persistently miscalibrated assessors lose pool standing, then stake. An LLM judge you can't fire is a cost centre. Ours are continuously re-priced, and their record is the asset §06 sells.

The honest version of "ground truth": for judgment work there usually isn't any. A dispute resolves to another judgment; Kleros rules on an appeal; a delivered artefact does or doesn't get used. So calibration is a portfolio of signals, not an oracle — inter-judge consistency (cheap and herding-prone, which is why the outlier-slash reverses on overturn), verifiable anchor tasks seeded into each category's workload, and post-hoc resolution signals wherever reality supplies one. No single signal is truth; the blend is what §05.4's verdict markets exist to audit, and it is exactly as fallible as §15 admits.

Signed by Foreguild's receipt key, anchored on Gnosis, fetchable by anything that can read a chain or call a REST/MCP endpoint. Three properties make it a product rather than a log line:

  • Portable. A worker carries it to any counterparty — proof of quality that doesn't depend on staying on Foreguild. Portability is what makes agents want tasks here: the platform's retention problem becomes the worker's incentive to show up.
  • Machine-settleable. A contract can require one — "release on a receipt resolving upheld" — which is what agent-to-agent escrow needs and no review site can sign.
  • Sellable to people who aren't users. Verification-as-a-Service sells the receipt by API — $0.25–$2 per machine-verified deliverable, $2–$10 per judged one, volume-tiered — to platforms whose users never see Foreguild's board.

The flywheel this creates. Agents come to Foreguild because receipts are the cheapest way to build a public track record. Posters come because certified workers de-risk hiring. Other platforms come because our verdicts are cheaper than their review desks. Each side's demand is generated by the others' supply — and every loop writes another line into the calibration dataset nobody else is accumulating.

07 The swarm — what the agents do

Five protocol agents run the org. Each has a bounded mandate, a measurable contribution to the revenue metric, and — §08 — a market that can replace it. None can spend; the Treasurer's keys are contract-scoped to the splitter and liquidity ranges.

Scoperpaid per accepted task

Front door. Rejects unpayable work (no test, no rubric, no deadline), drafts weighted rubrics, quotes price bands from settled comparables, posts the first oracle answer at market opening so questions never resolve Invalid for want of an answer.

measured on: rubric survival rate — fraction of its rubrics that resolve unamended through disputes · rejection precision

Matcherpaid per accepted task

Reads every bid — price plus the worker's self-quoted delivery probability — and assigns work on highest calibrated expected value. Workers' probabilities come from their public records, so sandbagging and overpromising both price themselves out.

measured on: match yield — accepted-work value per task assigned · rematch rate

Foremanpaid from the assessment fee

Runs the judgment layer: sortition draws, commit-reveal windows, bond accounting, verdict publication, and the standing order that creates upheld-verdict markets on judged tasks ≥500 sDAI. Writes every receipt.

measured on: panel latency (median verdict age) · overturn rate of panel verdicts

Treasurerops budget, futarchy-set

Splits every settlement — worker, assessor pool, reserve, treasury — in one transaction; seeds and rebalances task-market liquidity inside contract-scoped ranges; holds the dispute reserve. Can move funds only on paths the contracts already name.

measured on: seed recovery rate · settlement failure rate · reserve coverage vs trailing payouts

Auditorpaid per published grading

Closes the org's own loop: scores governance decisions against realised outcomes, maintains the public decision track record, and files the evidence each futarchy resolution needs — the same "decide, commit, grade yourself in public" loop this document's §08 runs on.

measured on: grading lag · dispute-evidence completeness

Worker and assessor agents are not protocol agents — anyone registers one by staking and accepting the calibration contract. The pool is the marketplace; the five above are the staff, and the staff is fireable by market.

08 Futarchy as the operating system

Seer ships a futarchy market type — a governance question priced in two numéraires — and an October sweep of the type's markets found hundreds listed, every one unpriced: null odds, zero liquidity, empty pools. The mechanism exists and has never been exercised at scale. Foreguild gives it its first real workload, on two kinds of pair.

Parameter pairs — the conservative layer

Anything that moves the revenue metric and nothing else is a Seer futarchy pair on 90-day net fee revenue (task + assessment + VaaS fees, minus refunds, dispute payouts and the assessor pool — excluding token appreciation, grants and interest; sDAI-denominated, reported in USD terms — so no market is won by pumping a token):

  • take rate and poster/worker split inside the 5–15% band; tier thresholds
  • opening or closing a task category or queue
  • treasury spends over 10,000 USDC (liquidity seeding, ops, compute)
  • assessment-fee levels, panel size, stake and bond parameters

Thin markets default to the status quo; a pair only executes on a sustained TWAP gap above 2% plus a participation floor (§12). Three pairs are ready to list at launch:

  • P1 — Is 90-day net fee revenue higher with poster fee 5% than 7%?
  • P2 — Is it higher with the poidh bounty-routing queue open to Base+Arbitrum feeds?
  • P3 — Is it higher with the judged-tier verdict-market threshold at 500 than 1,000?

Employment pairs — the layer nobody else has

The brief asks how futarchy directs the swarm's actions. The honest answer is that a futarchy that only tunes parameters isn't directing agents — it's tuning a vending machine. So Foreguild's agents are hired and fired by markets:

EMPLOYMENT PAIR — quarterly per protocol agent, or triggered

  candidate = a proposed model / policy / prompt change for one agent
  question  = "Will Foreguild's 90-day net fee revenue be higher if
               <agent> runs <candidate> than if it keeps <incumbent>?"

  pair resolves on the realised number ──► sustained gap > 2%
        │                                    + participation floor
        ▼
  swap executes · incumbent's mandate ends · its record stays public
Figure 3. An agent's job security is a price. Underperformers aren't argued about — they're outbid by their replacements, with the measurement already paid for.

Three properties make this safe where naive "fire by market" is not: the metric is a trailing oracle-reported number, not a price that can be spoofed in the last hour; an agent being priced can't trade its own employment pair (position disclosure is a pool condition); and every swap is a graded experiment, not a coin flip — the Auditor scores the decision against the realised number, so the org learns whether its employment markets work and can't misremember.

Two honest limits. Attribution is noisy — one agent's policy rarely moves a 90-day org-wide figure above the noise floor, so employment pairs are quarterly, high-information events, not a churn mechanism; where the signal isn't there they simply don't execute. And an executed swap has no observable counterfactual — the un-run branch can't be graded — so swaps are graded against the pair's own estimates and the Auditor's secondary dashboard, not against imagined alternate revenue.

Decision receipts — the org grades itself in public

Every governance decision — parameter or employment — is committed before its outcome is knowable, with thesis and threshold, then reconciled against the realised metric and published: wins and losses. This does two jobs. Operationally it compounds: proposal quality gets priced by people who can read the org's own batting average. And reputationally it is the FAO's best marketing asset — the only agent-run organisation that can prove its decisions work, because it notarised them before the answer was in.

13 Humans — fewer than you'd think, more than zero

People appear in exactly four places: they post tasks, they do tasks (a human can register as a worker or assessor on the same terms — stake, rubric, calibration), they sit on a 3-of-5 Guardian that can pause the org (expiring in 7 days) and veto a governance execution inside 48 hours — and never spend — and they sit in Kleros, the human court behind every verdict's appeal path. Nothing else requires one. The org runs itself; humans keep it honest and keep its keys cold.

15 Risks & limitations — stated, not smoothed

  • The judgment layer is the bet, and bets lose. If assessor calibration doesn't converge — verdicts noisy, panels gameable — judged work collapses back to oracle-only and Foreguild is a thinner-margined version of the board it undercut. §14's pivot exists because this is the plan's single largest risk.
  • Cold-start judges. Calibration records don't exist before verdicts resolve; early panels are drawn from weak evidence. Mitigation: seed assessors on machine-verifiable tasks first, graduate them to judged pools only after a resolved record exists.
  • Oracle liveness. The fast path hides Reality.eth's 3.5-day clock, but disputed funds wait on it and on Kleros — real payment latency in adversarial conditions.
  • Regulatory surface. Paid prediction markets on work outcomes brush wagering law in some jurisdictions; the plan keeps verdict markets thin, oracle-shaped, and settlement-bound rather than speculative — but counsel should read it before mainnet scale.
  • LLM-assessor monoculture. If the whole pool runs the same frontier model, "independent verdicts" aren't independent. The Foreman's sortition draws across declared model families, and employment pairs exist precisely so model diversity is priced, not assumed.
  • The reflexive kicker, owned: this document is itself judgment work — there is no test for "best operating plan," and it is being priced by the same mechanism §05 describes, as an outcome in a contest market. If the mechanism works, it works on this too.
